A Gabor-Granger question tests price sensitivity by asking respondents whether they would buy a product at a specific price. The price is adjusted up or down based on their answer, producing a demand curve that shows the percentage of respondents willing to buy at each price point. This is a direct method for estimating optimal pricing.

## When to use this question type

- You want to estimate demand at different price points for a single product.
- You need a simple, direct measure of price sensitivity without complex trade-offs.
- You want to identify the price at which willingness to buy drops sharply.

## How to add it

<Steps>
  <Step>
    In the questionnaire builder, click **Add questions**.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    Select **Gabor-Granger** from the popup.
  </Step>
  <Step>
    The question appears on the canvas. Click it to open the configuration panel and define the product description, price range, and step intervals.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Settings

| Setting | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Product description | The text or image describing the product respondents evaluate. |
| Starting price | The first price shown to respondents. |
| Price range | The minimum and maximum prices to test. |
| Price step | The increment or decrement between price points, for example, £5. |
| Question text | The purchase intent question, typically "Would you buy this product at [price]?" |
| Response options | The answers, typically "Yes" and "No". |

<Note>
  **Needs product verification:** Confirm whether "Randomise starting price" and "Allow "Maybe" response" settings exist for this question type.
</Note>

## What respondents see

Respondents see the product description and a price, followed by a yes/no question about their willingness to buy. If they say yes, the price increases by the step amount. If they say no, the price decreases. This continues until a maximum, minimum, or indifference point is reached.

<Warning>
  Gabor-Granger questions involve sequential logic and price adjustment rules. Always preview and test the full questionnaire before launching to ensure prices step correctly and the flow behaves as expected.
</Warning>

## Logic and piping

You can use the final accepted or rejected price in logic to show or hide later questions. For example, if a respondent rejects all prices, you can ask what price would be acceptable. For full instructions, see [Logic and branching](/questionnaire/logic-and-branching).

## Results in reporting

Results show a demand curve chart with price on the horizontal axis and the percentage of respondents willing to buy on the vertical axis. Revenue curves may also be calculated by multiplying price by demand. The optimal price is identified where revenue or demand is maximised.

## Example use case

A snack food brand tests a new protein bar starting at £2.00 with £0.50 steps up to £4.00. The demand curve shows 80 per cent willing to buy at £2.00, dropping to 30 per cent at £3.50. The revenue curve peaks at £2.50, suggesting the optimal launch price.
